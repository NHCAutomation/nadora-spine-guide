Introduction

Spinal (back) surgery is the most common procedure performed today. Over one million spinal procedures are performed a year. The purpose of spine surgery is to help relieve pain, numbness or tingling, and other symptoms that patients may be experiencing. It is one approach to helping improve the quality of life for patients.

Patient Rights

As a patient, you have the right to be informed about and make decisions regarding your care. You also have the right to care that is free from discrimination, as well as the right to have a patient advocate.

As a patient, you have the right to:

  • Be informed about your care.
  • Make decisions about your care.
  • Refuse care.
  • Know the names of your caregivers.
  • Be treated with courtesy and respect.
  • Be listened to by your caregivers.
  • Have an interpreter.
  • Receive information in a way that meets your needs, such as if you have impaired vision.
  • Religious or spiritual services.
  • Copies of your test results and medical records.
  • Have a patient advocate with you during your care.
  • Privacy of your health information.
  • Ask that pictures or videos taken of you be used only to identify you or assist in your care.
  • Care that is free from discrimination.

Be Active in Your Care

Ask questions

You are always welcome and encouraged to ask questions regarding your care. Here are some common questions you can ask:

  1. What procedure am I having done?
  2. What do I need to do to prepare for surgery?
  3. Is there any medication I need to stop or start taking before the procedure?
  4. Is there anything I need to bring with me the day of surgery?

Anatomy

The spine is made up of the following 3 major components:

  • The spinal column (bones and discs)
  • The neural elements (spinal cord and nerve roots)
  • Supporting structures (muscles and ligaments)

The spinal column consists of 7 cervical vertebrae (C1–C7), 12 thoracic vertebrae (T1–T12), 5 lumbar vertebrae (L1–L5), the sacrum, and the coccyx.

Preparing for Surgery

Preparing for surgery may begin several weeks before the surgery itself, and it is important to plan ahead. You may be directed to complete the following:

Physical exam

Your primary care doctor can identify conditions that may interfere with surgery and/or recovery.

Lab tests

You may need to have routine lab tests done. Tests may include:

  • Blood tests
  • Urine tests
  • EKG or cardiogram
  • Chest X-ray

Results from these are used in planning your care.

Dental exam

To help reduce the risk of infection, all dental work should be completed before surgery. Infection occurs when bacteria enter the bloodstream. The risk for this is significantly higher with untreated dental issues such as gum disease or abscesses.

Medication review

Before your surgery, your doctor will review and educate you on which medications you need to stop or continue taking before surgery.

Preparing at Home

Here are some steps you can take to prepare your home:

  • Clear pathways by removing throw rugs and cords
  • If possible, have your bed near a bathroom
  • Organize and create an emergency plan with people you trust
  • Arrange for home health care

Recovery

What to expect after surgery

After surgery you will be cared for by a nurse who will monitor your vital signs and help you recover from anesthesia.

When stable, you will be transported to another recovery room for further observation. Depending on your specific recovery needs, you may be discharged that day or stay in the facility for 1–3 days.

Pain

It is normal to experience pain after surgery. A nurse will support you by managing your pain with I.V. pain medications and, once you are able to tolerate food and drink, gradually switch you to oral pain medications. They will guide you through this process with the use of a pain scale.

Rehabilitation

Physical therapy

Physical therapy may be an important part of your recovery. Some procedures may or may not require therapy.

As you become more mobile, you will be educated on post-operative activity modifications and limitations. Instructions on proper use of braces, adaptive equipment, and post-operative exercises will be given.

Occupational therapy

Depending on your specific needs and limitations, an occupational therapist may be needed. They will help you with the following:

  • Ways to bathe, dress, and do simple meal preparation.
  • Education on adaptive equipment such as reachers, walkers, and long-handled sponges.

Complications

Although uncommon, spine surgery patients are at risk for complications.

Infection

An infection may occur in the wound or the area around the wound. There are many precautions used in the operating room to minimize this risk. Your doctor may also prescribe antibiotics to help prevent infection.

Warning signs of infection after surgery

Notify your physician immediately if you develop any of the following:

  • Persistent fever (over 101°F)
  • Shaking or chills
  • Increasing redness, tenderness, and swelling of the wound
  • Excessive cloudy drainage from the wound
  • Increasing pain with activity and rest
  • Positional headache (that goes away after lying flat)
  • Photophobia (sensitivity to light)
  • Persistent nausea and vomiting

Preventing Complications

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

These are blood clots that commonly develop in the lower leg. Various measures are taken to reduce the possibility of DVTs, such as:

  • Wearing special support hose or compression socks
  • Blood-thinning medications
  • Ankle pump exercises
  • Walking

Warning signs of DVT after surgery

Notify your physician immediately if you develop any of the following:

  • Increased pain in the calf (unrelated to the incision)
  • Tenderness or redness of your calf
  • Excessive swelling of the calf, ankle, or foot
  • Sudden shortness of breath
  • Sudden onset of chest pain (with coughing)

Life After Spinal Surgery

The recovery process takes from six weeks to a year. Spinal precautions are often discontinued during this time, but the healing process continues for up to one year after surgery. Your doctor will recommend the appropriate level of activity following spine surgery.

The majority of people who have spine surgery experience a reduction in pain and an improvement in their ability to perform activities of daily living. Enjoy your new quality of life after healing!

Activities that should be avoided

  • Activities causing high-impact stress on the spine
  • Activities with a high risk of injury
  • Activities with a risk of falling
